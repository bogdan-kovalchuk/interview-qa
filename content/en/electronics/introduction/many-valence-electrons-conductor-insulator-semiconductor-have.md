## Short answer

In a simplified model, a metallic conductor is often cited as having 1–3 loosely bound valence electrons, silicon as a semiconductor has 4, and many insulators have a filled outer shell.[^aac-conductors-valence] This is merely an educational approximation: conductivity is also determined by crystal structure and available energy states, so the number of electrons alone is insufficient to classify a material.[^aac-conductors-valence]

## Detailed explanation

The number of valence electrons does not form a universal rule for classifying conductors, insulators, and semiconductors: electrical conductivity depends on the energy states of electrons and the bonds within the material.[^aac-conductors-valence] In the basic model, silicon has four valence electrons that form covalent bonds with neighboring atoms; for metals, one speaks in simplified terms about electrons available for motion, whereas in many insulators electrons remain bound.[^aac-semiconductors]

Therefore, the assertion "a conductor has this many electrons, and an insulator has that many" is not a general law. For example, graphite and diamond are both composed of carbon atoms with the same number of valence electrons, but have different conductivity due to differences in bonding structure.[^aac-conductors-valence]

Example: undoped silicon is a semiconductor; adding donors or acceptors alters carrier concentration, and consequently the electrical behavior of the crystal.[^aac-semiconductors]

In a metal, electrons can occupy states that allow them to respond to an electric field and transport charge; in an insulator under normal conditions, there are far fewer such available states. In a semiconductor, the number of carriers can be noticeably altered by temperature, illumination, or doping. Thus, counting the electrons in the outer shell is useful for initial intuition, but making a prediction also requires the band model and bonding structure of the specific material.[^aac-conductors-valence]
