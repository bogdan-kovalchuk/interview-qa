## Short answer

The coulomb (symbol C) is the unit of electric charge. The charge of an electron equals `−1.602176634 × 10⁻¹⁹ C`, so in magnitude 1 C corresponds to approximately `6.24 × 10¹⁸` electrons.[^nist-elementary-charge]

## Detailed explanation

The coulomb measures charge, not the number of particles. The elementary charge `e` is defined exactly: its magnitude is equal to `1.602176634 × 10⁻¹⁹ C`; an electron has a charge of `−e`, and a proton – `+e`.[^nist-elementary-charge]

To estimate the number of electrons, one must divide the magnitude of the total charge by the magnitude of the charge of a single electron. Thus, one coulomb in magnitude corresponds to approximately `6.24 × 10¹⁸` electrons. The value is approximate only because the result is rounded; the elementary charge itself is exact in the SI.[^nist-elementary-charge]

This relationship describes net charge. In an ordinary conductor, electrons move in both directions and can be very numerous, but their contribution to the measurable charge is determined by an excess or deficiency of electrons, not by the total quantity of electrons in the material.

The sign of the charge cannot be ignored either: an excess of electrons yields a negative charge, whereas their deficiency relative to the neutral state – a positive charge. The formula for the number of particles uses the magnitude of charge because the count of electrons cannot be negative. If a calculation yields a non-integer number, that is typically the result of rounding or indicates that the given value is an average for a flow or measurement rather than an exact count of discrete electrons.[^nist-elementary-charge]

**Common mistake:** calling the coulomb a unit for the count of electrons. The coulomb is a unit of charge; the count of electrons is obtained from charge only if the charge is carried by electrons.
