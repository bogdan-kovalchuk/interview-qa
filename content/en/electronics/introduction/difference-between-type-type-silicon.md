## Short answer

In Si, donor dopants such as phosphorus produce N-type material with electrons as the majority carriers; acceptor dopants such as boron produce P-type material with holes as the majority carriers. Overall, each doped crystal remains electrically neutral.[^mit-semiconductor-physics]

## Detailed explanation

N-type and P-type silicon are produced by doping – the controlled introduction of impurity atoms into the Si crystal lattice. In a group V donor dopant, such as phosphorus, four valence electrons participate in bonds with neighboring silicon atoms, while the fifth relatively easily becomes a mobile electron. Therefore, in N-type material, electrons are the majority – that is, more numerous – charge carriers.[^mit-semiconductor-physics]

A group III acceptor dopant, such as boron, has one fewer valence electron than needed for a complete set of bonds with neighboring Si atoms. The unoccupied site in the bond is described as a hole; electrons from neighboring bonds can move into this vacancy, causing the hole to travel through the crystal. In P-type material, holes are the majority carriers, although a small number of electrons also remains.[^mit-semiconductor-physics]

The letters N and P designate the sign of the majority mobile carriers – negative electrons or positive holes, not the net charge of the sample. Ionized dopants are fixed in the lattice and balance the charge of the carriers, so both materials are overall neutral. Depending on temperature, doping concentration, and other conditions, the concentrations of majority and minority carriers vary; claiming that "N has only electrons, P has only holes" would be an oversimplification.[^mit-semiconductor-physics]

**Brief comparison:** donor -> N-type -> majority carriers electrons; acceptor -> P-type -> majority carriers holes.
