## Short answer

Low-frequency rectifier ripple and transient load sags have different origins, while high-frequency switching noise originates from fast transitions such as switching regulators.[^ti-switching-regulator-fundamentals] Power traces possess parasitic resistance and inductance.[^fiore-inductance-dc] Rapid current steps induce transient voltages across them according to `V_L = L*di/dt`.[^fiore-ac-circuit-analysis] A single bulk capacitor cannot solve all three problems: ripple and sags require high capacitance, whereas high-frequency suppression depends on low ESL and short trace inductance.[^ti-switching-regulator-fundamentals]

## Detailed explanation

Ripple is the variation in output voltage caused by the filter capacitor discharging between rectified AC voltage peaks.[^libretexts-fiore-rectification] In a full-wave bridge, the load receives unipolar current pulses during both half-cycles, so the ripple frequency is twice the power-line frequency: 100 Hz on a 50 Hz line or 120 Hz on a 60 Hz line.

Voltage sag during a load transient is a distinct phenomenon. The output capacitor supplies transient current while the upstream regulator has not yet reacted. Following `i = C*dv/dt`, the voltage across the capacitor changes at a rate of `i/C`,[^fiore-ac-circuit-analysis] meaning a larger capacitance reduces the magnitude of the sag, but cannot instantaneously replace the current that the supply delivers with a finite response delay.

High-frequency noise is generated by fast-switching circuit elements: transistors and diodes in switching converters generate substantial EMI that readily couples into PCB traces and wiring.[^ti-switching-regulator-fundamentals] A bulk electrolytic capacitor is ineffective here because its equivalent series inductance (ESL) severely limits its filtering capability at high frequencies.[^ti-switching-regulator-fundamentals] Consequently, high-frequency noise is suppressed using ceramic or film capacitors positioned directly adjacent to the load.

This is where conductor inductance becomes critical. A conductor opposes changes in the current flowing through it,[^fiore-inductance-dc] and according to `v = L*di/dt`, a rapid change in current across an inductance induces a voltage drop.[^fiore-ac-circuit-analysis] For illustration, consider a trace section with an inductance of 10 nH carrying a current step of 1 A in 10 ns: `V = L*di/dt = 10e-9 H * (1 A / 10e-9 s) = 1 V`. This transient voltage appears along the trace between the capacitor and the IC; therefore, a bulk capacitor located far away cannot support rapid current changes during such fast edges.

**Common mistakes:**

- Assuming that a single bulk capacitor simultaneously eliminates ripple, transient sags, and high-frequency noise.
- Placing bypass capacitors far from the IC pins: long traces possess parasitic inductance, leaving inductive `L*di/dt` transients unattenuated.
- Confusing low-frequency power-line ripple with high-frequency noise and attempting to filter both with the same capacitor technology.
